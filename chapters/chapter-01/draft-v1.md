# CHAPTER ONE

Anna awakes thinking, as she almost always is, about water. Her mouth is dry as chalk and her tongue feels too large for it, and some animal part of her brain is already reaching for a glass that isn't there.

She surfaces slowly. The room resolves around her in pieces: a low ceiling of dark timber, a narrow window with the curtain not quite closed, a wedge of grey morning light lying across a quilt she doesn't recognize. For a moment she doesn't know where she is, only that she traveled a long way to get here and that her body has not forgiven her for it yet. Then it comes back to her that she is in the Yulaevs' guest room, in a village in Bashkortostan.

She sits up carefully, the way you do in an unfamiliar bed, testing the give of the mattress before committing her weight to it. Somewhere below her, through floorboards that don't quite meet the walls, she can hear the low domestic clatter of a kitchen already awake — a pan set down, water running, a child's voice asking something in Bashkir that gets answered in the same language, too fast for her to follow.

She finds a glass on the nightstand she hadn't noticed in the dark last night. Someone left it for her. The water in it is room temperature, a little flat, and she drinks all of it anyway, in three long swallows, before she lets herself think about anything else.

Only then does she reach for the notebook, which is on the nightstand too, where she put it before sleep, out of habit more than intention — she does this everywhere, hotel rooms, layovers, the back of trucks. It's small and black, its cover softened with age and handling, and it is Lev Antonov's, not hers. She has never quite let herself think of it as hers, even six years on, even though no one else would know what to do with it and no one else has asked for it back.

She doesn't open it, only holds it a moment, the way you'd hold something to check it's still there, and then she gets up to face the day.

The stairs are steep enough that she takes them sideways, a hand on the wall. The kitchen at the bottom is warm and smells of fried dough and woodsmoke, and it's smaller than it sounded from upstairs. A woman in a cardigan is at the stove with her back to the door. A boy of perhaps six sits at the table with a spoon in his fist, watching the doorway as if he has been waiting for Anna specifically and is a little disappointed by what has arrived.

"Good morning," Anna says, in Russian.

The woman turns, smiles, and points her at a chair with the spatula. "Sit. Eat. Your car comes at eight."

There's already a plate of fried bread in flat golden circles, with a saucer of sour cream and a jar of honey so dark it looks like engine oil until the light catches it. Tea arrives without anyone asking whether she wants any. Anna says thank you twice, and the second one is ignored as excessive.

The boy says something to his mother in Bashkir. His mother answers without turning around. Whatever it is, it makes him put his spoon down and stare at Anna with fresh interest.

"He asks if you are the water lady," the woman says.

Anna considers this. Last night, climbing out of the Deka van in the rain with her bags, she said very little to anyone, and nothing at all about her work. Someone at the station must have told them who was coming.

"I suppose I am," she says.

The boy nods, satisfied, and goes back to his breakfast. Apparently that settles it.

The honey is ferocious. It tastes of smoke and something bitter underneath, like flowers that grew somewhere difficult, and she eats more of it than she means to while the woman tells her, without being asked, that it comes from her husband's cousin, who keeps hives in the old way, in the trees, up past the reserve where nobody is supposed to go. Anna asks how anyone gets up there, then. The woman shrugs, as if the question doesn't quite apply to cousins.

At eight exactly a horn sounds in the lane. The woman wraps two more pieces of fried bread in a napkin and puts them in Anna's hand before she can protest. At the door, the boy says something else in Bashkir, very seriously, and doesn't translate it himself.

"He says don't fall in," his mother says.

***

The car is a white Deka pickup with a rack of antennae on the cab and mud to the door handles. The driver is young, with a shaved head and a sunburned neck, and he takes her bags from her and throws them into the back without asking which one has the instruments in it. She winces, and he doesn't notice.

There is already someone in the back seat, a heavy, gray-haired man in a fleece with a conference lanyard still around his neck, from some other year, some other city. He shakes her hand across the seat and tells her his name, which she forgets at once, and that he is a geophysicist from Novosibirsk, which she keeps.

"They put you in a house too?" he says.

"With a family. Yulaev."

"Mine has a dog that hates me. Personally. It doesn't bark at the others." He seems almost proud of it. "Three days now. I tried sausage. Nothing."

Anna says she's sorry about the dog. He says the dog is the least of it, and starts telling her about the camp toilets, and she lets him, because it means she doesn't have to talk, and turns to the window.

The road follows the river up the valley. For a while it's a proper road, tarmac patched and repatched until it's more patch than road, and then it isn't. The pickup drops onto packed gravel and the whole cab begins to shiver. On her side the river keeps them company, sometimes right below the verge and sometimes farther off through birch and alder, and every time it reappears she finds she has been waiting for it.

It is a beautiful river. That's the first thing, and it annoys her a little, because she has seen a great many rivers and is not supposed to be moved by them anymore. It runs fast and shallow over pale stones, braiding and unbraiding around gravel bars, and in the slack water at the edges it's so clear she can see the shadows of the stones on the bottom. In August, after a dry month, that alone means nothing. Plenty of mountain rivers look like this in late summer. You could pour it in a glass and photograph it for an advertisement, and it would tell you nothing at all about what was in it.

At home, the rivers never looked like anything. The Pina and the Pripyat lay brown and wide and slow in their marshes, with peat in them and the tea-colored stain of all that bog, and you never once in your life saw the bottom.

The man from Novosibirsk has moved on from the toilets to the food. The driver turns the radio up a notch, not quite rudely.

She thinks, because there's nothing to stop her thinking it on a road like this, about the contract in her bag. It's a better contract than anyone has offered her in a long time, and she knows why. Deka wanted a hydrologist with field experience in wet, difficult, post-Soviet landscapes who would sign a nondisclosure agreement without reading it twice. She read it four times and signed it anyway. She tells herself it's because the money is real and the science might be, and because at her age you don't get many more chances to be in the room where something gets measured for the first time. Some of that is even true.

The valley narrows and the river cuts in close under the road, and then the trees fall back and the station is there.

It sits on a terrace above the water where the valley widens briefly before closing again, and her first impression is of a place that has been dead for a long time and has only recently been told otherwise. There's the old station itself, a long, low block of Soviet concrete gone the color of wet ash, half its windows boarded and half replaced with new glass that looks too clean, as if someone had fitted false teeth to an old man. Below it, at the river, a concrete weir with a gauge house squatting on one end, a rusted cableway strung across the water above it. Around all of it, and outnumbering it, Deka has set down rows of white prefabricated units on blocks, a humming generator shed, shipping containers painted in the company's blue and stacked on one another, and a mast with a dish on it, and has run a tall new wire fence around the lot, with a gate, a guard hut, and a sign Anna can't read yet but can guess at.

"Home sweet home," says the man from Novosibirsk.

The driver laughs, the first sound she's heard him make, and swings the pickup in toward the gate.

***

Intake takes place in a prefab unit by the gate that has been fitted out as a clinic, a post office, and a passport control all at once. There are plastic chairs in rows, a water cooler with no cups, and a laminated poster of the company's values on the wall, which Anna reads twice while she waits because there's nothing else to read. *Responsibility. Clarity. Endurance.* Someone has drawn a small, competent cat in the margin beside *Endurance* in biro.

The woman at the desk takes her photograph against a white sheet and prints her a badge while she watches, still warm. *MOROZOVA A. — SCIENCE (W2)*. Her personal phone is fitted with a sticker over the camera lens. Nobody explains this. She is told she may use the station network, but not to photograph anything beyond the residential units, and that her laptop will be returned to her by the end of the day, once it has been "configured." She signs a form saying she has understood. She signs another saying she has received her badge, and a third whose purpose she can't work out even after reading it.

Then she is handed a map of the station on stiff card. The residential blocks are colored yellow, the laboratories green, the river access points blue. A good third of the map, mostly along the water downstream of the weir and up into a side valley to the north, is hatched over in gray with no legend at all.

"What's gray?" she asks.

"Gray is gray," says the woman at the desk, kindly, as if this were helpful.

Behind Anna, two men in Deka overalls are waiting for something at the counter. One of them looks at her badge, and then at the man from Novosibirsk, who is having trouble with his photograph.

"Wave two," he says to his friend, not quietly. "The professors have arrived. Hide the good coffee."

"There isn't any good coffee."

"That's how well I hid it."

They both look pleased with this. The man from Novosibirsk, still blinking from the flash, asks them where the toilets are, and they tell him, and they are right about that much at least.

Anna is on her way out with her map and her warm badge when a young man with a tablet checks her name against a list and asks whether she would mind waiting, because Ms. Reinhardt would like a word. It is phrased as a question, and Anna answers it as one, but she has already been steered by the elbow toward a door at the end of the unit.

The office behind it is small and very tidy, and the woman behind the desk stands up when Anna comes in. That surprises her, slightly. Most people who have offices like this one don't stand up.

"Dr. Morozova. Thank you. Eva Reinhardt. Sit, please." She has a low voice and a faint accent Anna can't place, something northern European worn smooth by years of airports. She is perhaps fifty, gray-blonde, dressed for the field in clothes that have never been near a field. Her handshake is brief and dry. "I hope the Yulaevs fed you."

"Extremely."

"They do that. We've had people come back from those houses heavier than they left." She sits. "I won't keep you long. I like to meet the science people myself, when I can. Otherwise everything you know about us comes through the people at that counter, and they mean well, but."

She doesn't finish the sentence, and doesn't seem to need to. She turns her own tablet around on the desk so that Anna can see it. On the screen is the station's data pack, the one Anna will be given access to by tonight, Eva says, once IT has finished doing whatever IT does to laptops. She scrolls through it slowly, and Anna watches the pages go by: site maps, sampling schedules, and the wave-one baseline, which tracks temperature, flow, turbidity, and conductivity at a string of monitoring points along the river from the weir down. The graphs are clean and well made by somebody careful.

"You'll know better than me what you're looking at," Eva says. "I read your paper on the Polesie floodplain, by the way. The one about delayed return flow. I didn't understand all of it, but I understood that you had stood in the water for most of a year to write it, and I liked that."

Anna is not good at being complimented by people she has just met, and she has never once been complimented on that particular paper, which almost nobody has cited, and some of those unkindly. She says thank you. It comes out stiffer than she means.

"Here's what we have," Eva says. "And here's what we'd like you to look at."

The conductivity line is the first thing Anna's eye goes to, because it's the reason any of them are here. A limestone river ought to carry the mountain it runs through, dissolved, in every liter. That's simply what water does to limestone. The Shulgan, by the graph, is carrying almost none of it. Not at every point, and not always, but more often than not, and for long stretches, it reads like a river that has never touched rock.

She knew this. She read the summary in Minsk, and again on the train. Seeing it laid out site by site is something else.

"It doesn't do that," she says, mostly to herself.

"No," Eva agrees. "That's what everyone says."

Anna scrolls on with one finger, the way she would through anyone's data, looking for the part they'd rather she didn't look at. She finds it a few pages in. The monitoring points run in a neat sequence downstream of the weir, numbered, each with its own small graph, and then the numbers skip. Four sites have no graph at all. There are just their numbers and a gray cell where the data should be, the same gray as the hatching on her map.

"These ones," she says.

"Access," says Eva. "Some of the river is still being cleared. Old infrastructure, permissions, safety. You'll have everything you need for your work as it becomes available."

It's a good answer, polished from use, and it answers nothing, and Anna has the feeling that Eva knows exactly how little it answers and doesn't mind her knowing. There's no unkindness in it. If anything there's something like respect, the way one professional acknowledges another's technique.

Further on, between a table of rainfall and a map of the old Soviet borehole network, there's a single page Anna doesn't expect: a regional table from a public health survey, rates of age-related illness in the districts along the river, set against the national figures. The district numbers are lower, not wildly but consistently. Someone has highlighted the column in pale yellow and written nothing beside it. She looks at it for a moment and moves on, because it isn't her field, and because she has learned that a person who comments on everything gets listened to about nothing.

"Questions?" Eva says, when she has reached the end.

Anna has a great many. She asks about the sampling intervals, and whether wave one's instruments were cross-calibrated against an independent lab, and how soon she can put her own probes in the water. Eva answers each one directly and well. When she doesn't know something, she says so and writes it down. It is, Anna thinks, a very good performance of having nothing to hide, unless it isn't a performance at all, and she can't tell which, and that bothers her more.

"Thank you, Dr. Morozova," Eva says, standing again at the end. "I'm very glad you're here. I mean that. This river needs people who'll be stubborn about it."

Anna can't decide, walking back out past the poster and the cat and the man from Novosibirsk's second attempt at a photograph, whether that was meant as a welcome or a warning, or both, or whether Eva Reinhardt simply says it to everyone.

***

The safety briefing for wave two is held in the canteen at two o'clock, between lunch and whatever the canteen does in the afternoons, and it smells of both. The new arrivals sit at long tables while a man from operations clicks through slides about fire assembly points, bears, ticks, and the river. The river slide is the longest. It has a photograph of the weir in spring, white water over its whole width, and the word *NEVER* in red at the bottom, followed by a list of things never to do.

Anna sits near the end of a table by the window, because she always sits by windows, and because from there she can see the water.

She can also see the gauge house. It squats at the far end of the weir, a concrete box with a corrugated roof, and there is a man standing alone on the walkway beside it, when everyone else at the station, as far as she can tell, is in this room or in the laboratories or asleep. He has a notebook open on the railing and he's looking down at the water going over the lip of the weir, not writing, just looking, the way she looks at water when she's trying to think.

He is older than most of the people here, heavyset, in a canvas jacket that has been patched at the elbows by someone who knew how to patch. From this distance he could be a forestry worker, or someone's uncle who has come to fix the generator. While she watches, he crouches, reaches down through the railing, and lays his flat palm on the concrete of the weir for a long moment, as if he were feeling for a pulse.

"Ah," says the technician sitting next to her. He's one of the two from the counter, the one with the coffee joke. He has seen where she's looking. "You've found our consultant."

"Who is he?"

"Khasanov. Local geologist. Deka hired him for the permits, I think, back at the start. Knows everyone, knows every hole in every hill." He shrugs. "Sweet man. Don't get him started."

"On what?"

"Anything. Water spirits. The old days." He grins. "Ask him the time and he'll tell you about the Ice Age. We had him in the planning meetings for the first month. Then we stopped having him in the planning meetings."

"Why?"

The technician looks at her as if the answer were obvious, and then, finding that it isn't obvious to her, seems briefly unsure himself. "It slowed things down," he says.

On the screen, the man from operations has moved on to ticks. Anna looks back out of the window. The man on the weir has stood up again. He closes his notebook, puts it inside his jacket, and starts back along the walkway toward the bank, unhurried, the way people walk when they've decided long ago that very little is worth hurrying for.

When the briefing ends, and the arrivals spill out into the yard blinking at the sun, he is coming up the path from the river. He passes close by her. For a moment his eyes rest on her badge, then on her face, with an attention that is somehow neither friendly nor unfriendly, only thorough. He nods once, the small nod of a man acknowledging another person on a narrow path, and goes on up toward the old station block.


***

The last van down to the village leaves at seven. Before it, with her laptop still somewhere being configured and her own instruments still sealed in their crates pending some form she hasn't been given yet, Anna goes down to the river with the one thing nobody thought to take from her.

It's an old handheld meter, the kind with a probe on a coiled cable, and it has been with her since her doctorate. The case is cracked and mended with tape. The screen has a dead row of pixels across the top, so that every reading has to be read slightly from below, like a word with its hat pulled down. She has newer instruments, but this is the one she trusts.

The map in her pocket has a blue dot for a river access point below the residential units, downstream of the weir, and she follows the path to it through tall grass that soaks her trousers to the knee. The access point turns out to be a gravel bar and a wooden step someone has nailed into the bank. Not far beyond it, a length of red-and-white tape is strung between two stakes and the grass is uncut. On her map, the tape is where the blue stops and the gray begins.

She stands on the step for a while before she does anything. The evening light has gone long and yellow, and the river has changed color with it, from glass to something like weak tea held up to a lamp, gold over the pale stones. It's loud this close, in a cheerful, ordinary way. A wagtail is working the edge of the gravel bar, bobbing, uninterested in her. Out in the current a branch has caught on a stone and is shivering there, and she watches it, and the water folding round it, and the small standing wave it has raised, and it's a long moment before she notices that she is thinking about nothing at all. That almost never happens anymore. She lets it go on a little longer than she should.

At home, as a girl, she used to lie on her stomach on the plank jetties at the edge of the marsh and watch the water under them, which was brown and still and looked like it was going nowhere. And then she'd drop in a leaf and watch it travel off, slow and sure, carried by a current she couldn't see and couldn't feel with her fingers. Water is never standing still, her father told her once, even when it looks it. Especially when it looks it. She has built most of her career on that one sentence, and she has never told anyone where it came from.

She crouches on the step, uncoils the probe, rinses it in the current, and holds it down in the water where it runs fastest.

The number comes up, wavers, and settles. She reads it from below the dead pixels. She takes the probe out, rinses it, and does it again, and then a third time a meter downstream, and a fourth from the slack water at the edge of the bar, where it ought to be higher because still water picks up more of whatever is around it.

It comes out no higher, the same near enough, and every one of the readings is lower than anything in the data pack she was shown this morning, and lower than she would expect to get from rain.

She sits back on her heels. In her head, unhelpfully, she can see the graphs in Eva Reinhardt's tablet, all those careful clean lines, and the gray cells where the downstream sites should have been. She is standing, she realizes, about as close to the first of those sites as the tape will let her.

She puts her free hand into the river, because there's nothing else to do with it. The cold goes through her fingers to the bone at once, the honest cold of a mountain river, with nothing strange about it to look at or to touch. If she had not had the meter, she would have drunk it.

Something surfaces, then, that she has spent six years being careful not to think about: a lecture hall in Minsk with a projector whose fan rattled, and an old man at the front with a pointer, saying that the deep water under these mountains did not behave like water left to itself, while from the back of the hall came the sound of a room deciding to laugh.

She takes her hand out of the river and dries it on her knee.

She could log the readings now. The meter can't send anything, but her phone can, over the station network, with its camera taped over. There's a form for it on the Deka system. Someone showed her where it was this afternoon. She takes the phone out and looks at it, and puts it back.

Instead she finds, in her jacket pocket, the napkin the woman at the Yulaev house wrapped the fried bread in this morning. The bread is long gone, eaten in the pickup somewhere along the valley while the man from Novosibirsk talked about the food. The napkin is soft and translucent with grease. She smooths it on her knee, finds a pencil, and writes the four readings down, small, with the time and the place, the way she was taught to write everything down, the way she would have written it for anyone.

She looks at it for a long moment. Then she folds it, and folds it again, and crumples it in her fist, and pushes it down into the bottom of her pocket, under the map, where her hand can find it.

Up at the station, somebody leans on the horn of the seven o'clock van, early, twice.
