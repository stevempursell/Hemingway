
***

Intake takes place in a prefab unit by the gate that has been fitted out as a clinic, a post office, and a passport control all at once. There are plastic chairs in rows, a water cooler with no cups, and a laminated poster of the company's values on the wall, which Anna reads twice while she waits because there's nothing else to read. *Responsibility. Clarity. Endurance.* Someone has drawn a small, competent cat in the margin beside *Endurance* in biro.

The woman at the desk takes her photograph against a white sheet and prints her a badge while she watches, still warm. *MOROZOVA A. — SCIENCE (W2)*. Her personal phone is fitted with a sticker over the camera lens. Nobody explains this. She is told she may use the station network, but not to photograph anything beyond the residential units, and that her laptop will be returned to her by the end of the day, once it has been "configured." She signs a form saying she has understood. She signs another saying she has received her badge, and a third whose purpose she can't work out even after reading it.

Then she is handed a map of the station on stiff card. The residential blocks are colored yellow, the laboratories green, the river access points blue. A good third of the map, mostly along the water downstream of the weir and up into a side valley to the north, is hatched over in gray with no legend at all.

"What's gray?" she asks.

"Gray is gray," says the woman at the desk, kindly, as if this were helpful.

Behind Anna, two men in Deka overalls are waiting for something at the counter. One of them looks at her badge, and then at the man from Novosibirsk, who is having trouble with his photograph.

"Wave two," he says to his friend, not quietly. "The professors have arrived. Hide the good coffee."

"There isn't any good coffee."

"That's how well I hid it."

They both look pleased with this. The man from Novosibirsk, still blinking from the flash, asks them where the toilets are, and they tell him, and they are right about that much at least.

Anna is on her way out with her map and her warm badge when a young man with a tablet checks her name against a list and asks whether she would mind waiting, because Ms. Reinhardt would like a word. It is phrased as a question, and Anna answers it as one, but she has already been steered by the elbow toward a door at the end of the unit.

The office behind it is small and very tidy, and the woman behind the desk stands up when Anna comes in. That surprises her, slightly. Most people who have offices like this one don't stand up.

"Dr. Morozova. Thank you. Eva Reinhardt. Sit, please." She has a low voice and a faint accent Anna can't place, something northern European worn smooth by years of airports. She is perhaps fifty, gray-blonde, dressed for the field in clothes that have never been near a field. Her handshake is brief and dry. "I hope the Yulaevs fed you."

"Extremely."

"They do that. We've had people come back from those houses heavier than they left." She sits. "I won't keep you long. I like to meet the science people myself, when I can. Otherwise everything you know about us comes through the people at that counter, and they mean well, but."

She doesn't finish the sentence. She doesn't seem to need to.

She turns her own tablet around on the desk so that Anna can see it. On the screen is the station's data pack, the one Anna will be given access to by tonight, Eva says, once IT has finished doing whatever IT does to laptops. She scrolls through it slowly, and Anna watches the pages go by: site maps, sampling schedules, the wave-one baseline. Temperature, flow, turbidity, conductivity, at a string of monitoring points along the river from the weir down. The graphs are clean and well made. Somebody has been careful.

"You'll know better than me what you're looking at," Eva says. "I read your paper on the Polesie floodplain, by the way. The one about delayed return flow. I didn't understand all of it, but I understood that you had stood in the water for most of a year to write it, and I liked that."

Anna is not good at being complimented by people she has just met, and she has never once been complimented on that particular paper, which almost nobody has cited, and some of those unkindly. She says thank you. It comes out stiffer than she means.

"Here's what we have," Eva says. "And here's what we'd like you to look at."

The conductivity line is the first thing Anna's eye goes to, because it's the reason any of them are here. A limestone river ought to carry the mountain it runs through, dissolved, in every liter. That's simply what water does to limestone. The Shulgan, by the graph, is carrying almost none of it. Not at every point, and not always, but more often than not, and for long stretches, it reads like a river that has never touched rock.

She knew this. She read the summary in Minsk, and again on the train. Seeing it laid out site by site is something else.

"It doesn't do that," she says, mostly to herself.

"No," Eva agrees. "That's what everyone says."

Anna scrolls on with one finger, the way she would through anyone's data, looking for the part they'd rather she didn't look at. She finds it a few pages in. The monitoring points run in a neat sequence downstream of the weir, numbered, each with its own small graph, and then the numbers skip. Four sites have no graph at all. There are just their numbers and a gray cell where the data should be, the same gray as the hatching on her map.

"These ones," she says.

"Access," says Eva. "Some of the river is still being cleared. Old infrastructure, permissions, safety. You'll have everything you need for your work as it becomes available."

It's a good answer. It's polished from use, and it answers nothing, and Anna has the feeling that Eva knows exactly how little it answers and doesn't mind her knowing. There's no unkindness in it. If anything there's something like respect, the way one professional acknowledges another's technique.

Further on, between a table of rainfall and a map of the old Soviet borehole network, there's a single page Anna doesn't expect: a regional table from a public health survey, rates of age-related illness in the districts along the river, set against the national figures. The district numbers are lower. Not wildly, but consistently. Someone has highlighted the column in pale yellow and written nothing beside it. She looks at it for a moment and moves on, because it isn't her field, and because she has learned that a person who comments on everything gets listened to about nothing.

"Questions?" Eva says, when she has reached the end.

Anna has a great many. She asks about the sampling intervals, and whether wave one's instruments were cross-calibrated against an independent lab, and how soon she can put her own probes in the water. Eva answers each one directly and well. When she doesn't know something, she says so and writes it down. It is, Anna thinks, a very good performance of having nothing to hide. Or it isn't a performance at all. She can't tell, which bothers her more.

"Thank you, Dr. Morozova," Eva says, standing again at the end. "I'm very glad you're here. I mean that. This river needs people who'll be stubborn about it."

Anna can't decide, walking back out past the poster and the cat and the man from Novosibirsk's second attempt at a photograph, whether that was meant as a welcome or a warning. Possibly it was meant as both. Possibly Eva Reinhardt says it to everyone.
