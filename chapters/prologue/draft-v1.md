# PROLOGUE

## The White Vein

The runoff came down the cut in one gray, shouldering mass, loud enough that Timur had stopped hearing it as noise some time ago and now mostly felt it in his knees. He was kneeling on a ledge of wet limestone with the probe cable wrapped twice around his wrist. The department's previous probe had gone into the Belaya on a day very much like this one, and the replacement form had run to eleven pages, and Timur had filled in every one of them himself.

"Conductivity," Alina said behind him.

She had the field notebook. It lived in a freezer bag, and she held the bag by one corner, away from her body, the way you would hold something a dog had brought you.

"Four hundred twelve."

"Four-twelve." She wrote it down. "Temperature."

"Two point one."

"It's the end of April," she said. "My grandmother has onions up already."

"Your grandmother lives on the ninth floor."

"On the balcony. In boxes. They're thriving. They're doing better than I am." She tipped her hood back to glare at the sky, got rain in her eye for it, and pulled the hood down again. "Next."

"pH."

"Of course it is."

He worked down the list. It was a short list, and he had worked down it perhaps two hundred times since September: at culverts and drainage ditches, at the rust-colored seeps below the old pits outside Sibay, standing in water that stained his boots orange to the ankle, writing numbers that all said roughly the same thing. The Soviet Union had dug a great many holes in these mountains and walked away from most of them, and the holes were still leaking into everything downhill. His thesis was about how far that leakage traveled underground, and by which roads. His supervisor, Gulnara Rashitovna, wanted the third chapter by the middle of May. She had recently started ending her emails with a single period on a line by itself.

Above them the slope went up into birch and fir and low cloud. They had left Alina's mother's car on the forestry road an hour ago and come in on foot, past a reserve boundary sign that somebody had used for target practice, past a run of warning fence lying flat where the winter's snow had pressed it into the mud, past a concrete survey post with a number stenciled on it in paint so faded it might have been a date. Twice they had skirted sinkholes brimming with brown water, young birches standing up out of the middle of them as if they had been planted on the bottom and were patiently waiting for the water to leave. Then the channel itself: a cut in the limestone, ribbed along both walls, the runoff tearing through it and vanishing under a broad overhang at the far end. Someone in the last century had bolted steel mesh across the face of the overhang to keep it from falling on whoever came next. Most of the bolts had given up. The mesh hung down in a long orange sheet and shivered when the water hit it.

Neither of them was supposed to be here. The reserve wanted a permit for anyone who came off the forestry road, and the department wanted a supervisor on any fieldwork during the flood, and Timur had decided, somewhere around the third week of waiting for Gulnara Rashitovna to sign a form, that both requirements were really suggestions about the pace of science. Alina had agreed to come because he was paying the petrol and because she needed the field hours for her own certification. She had also agreed, she reminded him at regular intervals, because she was an idiot.

"Put it in your Deka report," she said now. "Community engagement. 'Engaged a colleague in hypothermia.'"

"I engaged a fisherman last quarter."

"You asked a fisherman for directions."

"He engaged with me for almost ten minutes. I got two paragraphs out of him."

The scholarship paid his rent and his stipend and, today, the petrol. In exchange he filled in a quarterly form with a box at the bottom labeled *Community Engagement*, which someone at Deka presumably read, or fed to something that read it for them. He had learned to write two paragraphs about anything.

He coiled the cable, stood with a hand on the wall, and shuffled a few meters upstream along the ledge for the second station. On this side the cut had shed a slab at some point, a great tilted plate of limestone lying half in the channel with a dark gap behind it where it had come away from the wall. Out of that gap came a side flow, entering the main current at an angle, and the side flow was clear in a way that took him a moment to accept. He could see the stones under it, gray and ocher and sharp-edged, as plainly as if he were looking down through a pane of glass laid on the bed of the stream. It came out into the flood and did not mix. For the better part of a meter it ran on beside the gray water as a ribbon of its own, with an edge to it, holding together the way a dye band holds in a chromatography column, until the current got its fingers in and smeared it away.

It should have been pretty. He found that he didn't like looking at it.

"Alina."

She came along the ledge, testing each step with her boot before trusting it, and looked where he was looking. For a while she didn't say anything, which for Alina was a considerable statement.

"Spring," she said at last.

"In flood? Out of limestone?" Karst water in a thaw should have been the color of weak tea and loaded with everything the mountain had to give: calcium, bicarbonate, silt, half a winter's rotted leaves. "Look at it."

"I'm looking at it. It's a spring. Somebody's old pipe, maybe. Take your sample."

He knelt again and fed the probe into the clear ribbon, and the conductivity reading, which had been sitting comfortably around four hundred, began to fall. Three hundred. Two hundred and something. Forty. Nine. Then a row of dashes, and then the screen went black and lit again with the manufacturer's logo, the cheerful little swoosh it showed when it was restarting.

"Come on," Timur said to it.

"What?"

"It reset."

"Moisture."

"Probably moisture." He waited through the boot sequence, which took long enough that he had time to think about the replacement form, and put the probe back into the gray water. Four hundred and nine. He moved it into the clear ribbon. The number wavered, began to slide, and the screen froze on a reading of six before he pulled the probe out to save it from itself.

"You told me the seal was fine," Alina said.

"The seal was fine yesterday."

He uncapped a sample vial from the case, held it in the clear flow until it filled and the bubbles stopped, capped it underwater, and dried it on his sleeve, which only made it wetter. He wrote on the label in grease pencil: the date, *T-14*, *side flow under slab*. Through the glass the water looked like nothing. He held it up against the gray sky and could find no grain in it at all.

"There," Alina said. "Interesting. Very interesting. You can be interested in it in the car." She pointed with the notebook, still in its bag, at a branch caught against one of the limestone ribs a little downstream. When they had arrived, the water had been running a hand's width below the branch. Now it was tugging at it. "It's coming up."

It was. He could hear it too, now that she'd pointed it out, a deeper note under the roar, as the whole valley's worth of snow found its way down to them.

But there was a thin mist lying on the clear water where it came out from behind the slab, a white breath sitting just above the surface, and the mist was moving the wrong way. The wind was coming down the cut, into their faces. The mist was coming toward them too, out of the gap, against nothing. Timur pulled off one glove and held his bare wet hand up near the dark opening, and the cold came out and pressed on his palm, steady as a fan.

"Is that a Khasanov thing?" Alina said. "You're doing a Khasanov thing with your hand."

Dr. Khasanov had come to the university twice that Timur knew of, as a guest, to lecture second-years who mostly used the hour to catch up on sleep. At one point he had stood at the front of the hall with his own hand raised, palm out, feeling for a draft that wasn't there, talking about caves that breathe, in front of a hundred students who were not looking at him. Timur had been looking. His notes were somewhere in the back of the notebook Alina was holding by its corner, along with a good deal about river spirits that he had also written down, because it seemed rude not to.

"It's breathing out," he said, and flexed his fingers in the cold. "There's a room back there. A big one."

"There's a room in my grandmother's flat," Alina said, "that will have thirty people in it at eight o'clock tonight, and a cake that is currently strapped into the back seat of my mother's car with a seatbelt. I would like to be in one of those two places. I would especially like to be in the second one, before the cake is."

"Twenty minutes."

"Ten."

"Fifteen. I just want to see where it comes from."

"Ten, and you carry the case back up to the road, and you tell my grandmother yourself why I'm late if I'm late."

"Done."

Timur tied a line off on one of the mesh bolts that still had a grip on the rock and leaned back on it until he believed it. Then he got down on his hands and knees in the clear, cold water and put his head into the gap behind the slab.

Behind him he heard Alina say something to the sky in Tatar that her grandmother would not have liked, and then the splash of her coming after him.

The gap behind the slab was wider than it had looked from outside and lower than he would have liked. For the first few meters he could crawl, the water up to his elbows, the sample case dragging on its strap behind him and knocking his heels whenever the current caught it. Then the ceiling came down and he had to go onto his belly, and the cold that the neoprene had been keeping politely at a distance came in through the zip at his chest all at once and took his breath. He lay there for a second with his mouth open, making a small noise he was glad no one could hear over the water. Then he went on.

Every few meters he stopped to clip a plastic arrow onto the line, pointing back the way they had come.

"How far?" Alina called. Her voice came to him flattened against the rock.

"Not far."

"You don't know that."

"No."

The ceiling dropped again, until there was perhaps a hand's width of air between the surface and the rock, and he had to turn his face sideways to keep his mouth and nose in it. His helmet lamp lit a rough gray ceiling a few centimeters from his eye, beaded with drops that shook every time he breathed. He pulled himself forward along the floor by his fingertips. The case scraped behind him. He thought, with great clarity, about the eleven-page form, and about whether the department would make him fill in another one for the probe if he drowned and it survived. Then the ceiling lifted a little, and a little more, and he could raise his head.

It took him a while to notice how quiet it had become. The roar of the channel had been behind them the whole way, a weight pressing up the passage after them, and somewhere in the low section it had shrunk to a drone and then to almost nothing. What he could hear now was his own breathing, loud and wet inside the hood, and the small slap of water against stone, and Alina behind him saying, under her breath and without much conviction, that she hated him.

The water had changed too. At the entrance it had already been clearer than the flood, but there had still been grains in it, flecks of silt drifting through the lamp beam like dust in a window. Here there were fewer. A meter on there were fewer still. By the time the passage widened enough for him to get up onto his knees he could see his own gloved hands resting on the bottom, every grain of gravel under them, as if there were no water over them at all. He found that he had stopped moving to look at them.

"Timur."

"Sorry."

He crawled the last stretch and came up into the chamber.

It was not large. His lamp found the far wall at perhaps eight meters, and the ceiling at a little more than his own height, and the floor was under water deep enough to come to his thighs when he stood. The walls were black limestone, wet and gleaming, and the water lay on the floor so still and so clear that for a moment the chamber seemed to have no floor at all, only stone going down and down under the lamp.

Alina came up beside him, breathing hard. Her lamp swung across his and away, and that was how they both saw it.

A band of pale stone ran across the floor of the chamber, under the water, from one wall nearly to the other. It was a little wider than his outspread arms and the color of candle wax, or of very old ivory, and clear water was moving over it. He could see the movement only because of the way the lamplight shifted on the pale surface underneath. Thin streams of it came out of hairline fractures in the black rock on either side of the band and crossed toward the walls, where they disappeared into the darker water at the edges.

He waded toward it. The cold came up through his boots before he reached it, a deeper cold than the cold he had been carrying in from the crawl, and when he crouched and held the back of his gloved hand just above the surface over the band, his knuckles began to ache.

His first thought was quartz, because white veins in black rock are nearly always quartz. But quartz has faces. It fractures and catches the light, and this didn't do either. His second thought was calcite, flowstone, the ordinary pale skin that limestone caves grow over everything given enough time and dripping water. But flowstone sits on top of the rock it covers, and this went into the rock. The limestone met it the way bread meets the crust, without a seam, as though one had grown around the other. It was layered, and the layers were smooth and folded over one another in long soft overlapping curves, like the skin his grandmother used to lift off boiled milk with a spoon. He knew a little mineralogy, which was enough to know that nothing he knew looked like this.

"Take the sample," Alina said quietly from behind him. She had not come any closer. "Take your sample, and let's go."

He put the probe into the clear flow above the band.

The conductivity went down and kept going down, the way it had outside, and he watched it this time instead of fighting it. Twelve. Four. One point something. The calcium channel, which in karst water should have been shouting, read zero, then eighty, then zero again, as if it had found something and lost it. Dissolved solids fell from three hundred to two and stayed there, trembling in the last digit. He laughed. It came out short and ugly and echoed off the ceiling, and he stopped.

"What," Alina said.

"There's nothing in it."

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know. It means there's nothing in it." Water that had come through a mountain of limestone with nothing of the limestone in it. He might as well have been holding the probe in the department's ultrapure tap, the one with the warning sign over it that people used for coffee anyway.

He had his free hand flat on the rock beside the band to steady himself, and that was where he first felt it. It was not a sound. It was a very slight trembling in the stone under his palm, so slight he thought at first that it was his own pulse, or his own shivering. Then it was more than that. On the surface of the water, under his lamp, fine ripples appeared in straight lines and began to move. They crossed one another at angles, set after set, until for a second or two the whole surface over the pale band was ruled into a lattice, a moving grid like the interference pattern from the laser demonstration in first-year physics. Then it smoothed away, and the water lay still and clear as if nothing had touched it.

Across the chamber Alina had stopped breathing loudly. He could hear that she had stopped.

"You felt that," she said.

"Yes."

"Then we're going."

The second one came before he could answer. It was deeper and longer, and this time he did not feel it in his hand first. He felt it in his chest, in the bone in the middle of his chest, a pressure that went through him and out the other side, so low that his ears barely registered it at all, though his teeth did. His ears popped. Both of them, together, the way they did in a lift.

Behind them the water in the passage roared.

They turned. The entrance they had crawled out of, which had been a low, quiet mouth with a hand's width of air above the water, was now churning. Gray water from the flood was pushing in through it in heavy surges, the clear water of the chamber clouding around the opening as the silt came in, and the line he had laid ran out into that and vanished.

"That wasn't happening," Timur said, stupidly.

"No." Alina was already moving.

He filled the second vial without really deciding to, holding it down in the clear flow over the band until it was full, and capped it. Through his glove the glass was so cold it hurt. He was shoving it into the case when the water in the chamber moved around his legs. For a moment all of it, the whole still floor of it, was flowing the other way, away from the entrance and toward the pale band, toward the far wall, hard enough to lean him forward. Then it stopped, and snapped back, and came at the entrance with a force that nearly took his feet from under him.

On the line, where it ran out into the churning passage, the little plastic arrows had swung sideways. Every one of them. They hung crosswise in the current, pointing at the walls, pointing nowhere.

Alina went down. She was a step ahead of him, reaching for the line, and the water took her legs out and she disappeared under it up to the shoulders and came up again spitting, one hand locked on the line and the other clawing at the wall. He got hold of her harness and hauled, and she found her feet, and swore at him, or at the cave, in two languages.

"Go," he said. "Go first. Keep your hand on the line."

The passage had closed down. Where there had been a hand's width of air in the low section there were now a few fingers, and the water in it was moving, shoving forward and then dragging back, so that going through it was like being passed back and forth between two people who couldn't agree. He turned his face up into the air pocket and pulled himself along the line, the case banging against his legs, the cold so complete now that he had stopped feeling it as cold and felt it only as slowness, as hands that didn't close when he told them to. Ahead of him Alina's boots kicked and stopped and kicked. Once he heard the thud of her shoulder hitting rock and a cry that the water swallowed.

The third one came in the worst of it, with his face pressed up against the ceiling and the water at his lips. He felt it in his ribs and his teeth and in the rock against his cheek, and in that moment he had the idea, which afterward he would not have said aloud to anyone, that the stone around him had shifted its weight, the way a large animal shifts in its sleep. Then the idea was gone and there was only the line in his hand and the next pull and the next.

Daylight came at them gray and sudden, and they came out under the slab into the rain.

The channel was roaring exactly as it had before. The branch on the limestone rib was underwater now. The birches up the slope moved in the wind. Alina crawled out into the shallows on her hands and knees and stayed there a while, her head hanging, breathing, and then she got up all at once and stood holding her left shoulder with her right hand.

"I'm fine," she said, before he could ask. "Don't."

He turned to look at the gap behind the slab.

The side flow was still coming out of it, into the main current, and it was gray. It was the same gray as everything else, full of the same silt, and it went into the flood without an edge and was lost at once. There was no ribbon. There was no mist.

He looked at it for a long time, until Alina said his name. Then he cut the line at the mesh bolt with his knife, because he could not bring himself to go back under the slab to reel it in, and stuffed the loose end into his pocket. The case was heavy on its strap. He could feel the two vials through the side of it, against his hip, colder than anything else he was carrying.

Alina was already climbing toward the road. "You owe me petrol," she said, without looking back, "and a shoulder, and an explanation for my grandmother. And it was twelve minutes. I counted."

The chemistry building at night smelled of floor polish and old radiators, and of something underneath both that had been spilled some time in the last century and had never entirely left. The watchman in the glass booth by the doors looked up from his little television long enough to recognize Timur, take in his wet hair and the case on his shoulder, and decide that none of it was his business. That was the arrangement they had arrived at over three years, and Timur was grateful for it.

He changed into the dry clothes he kept in his locker for the nights he missed the last bus. His hands still smelled of the cave. On the fourth floor the extraction fans were thudding away in the ducts overhead, the way they did every night until one, like someone in the attic moving furniture who could never decide where it should go. One of the fluorescent tubes over his bench buzzed. It had buzzed since the autumn. He had filed a request about it, and then a second request about the first one.

He started with the bench conductivity meter, because it was better than the probe and because he wanted, more than he wanted to admit, for the probe to have been wrong.

It read zero point zero six.

He looked at it for a while. Then he walked across the lab to the ultrapure system in the corner, the one with the warning sign that everyone ignored, and looked at the little display on its front, which reported on the quality of the cleanest water in the building. Zero point zero five eight. He came back. The sample from the side channel, which had spent five hours in a vial in a car on a forestry road and then a highway, read the same as water that had been through three cartridges and an ultraviolet lamp twenty seconds ago.

That should not have lasted. Water that pure doesn't stay pure. It takes carbon dioxide out of the air the moment it's exposed to it, and leaches whatever it can from the glass, and the number starts to climb almost as soon as you look at it. He knew this the way he knew his own phone number. He stood over the meter for ten minutes, with his arms folded, and watched the number not climb. He checked the meter against a standard. The meter was fine.

The mass spectrometer down the hall had arrived two years ago in a crate with Deka's logo on the side and a usage agreement that ran to five pages. It had a booking calendar he was supposed to fill in a week ahead. He did not fill it in. He prepared aliquots of both vials with his hands not quite steady, loaded them into the queue behind someone's abandoned soil digests, and went back to his bench to wait.

The vending machine on the landing had been dead since February. He ate a packet of crackers from the back of his drawer and opened the Deka quarterly report, and looked at the box at the bottom for some time, and typed *Engaged* and deleted it. His phone buzzed. Alina had sent a photograph of the cake, intact, with her grandmother's hand cutting into it and a lot of people's elbows around the edges. A minute later a second message came: *shoulder purple. you owe me.* And a minute after that: *what was it*

He wrote *don't know yet* and then sat looking at the words until the screen went dark.

The trace results came through a little after half past eleven. He had a spreadsheet of more than two hundred samples from this district, collected since September, and every one of them carried the same fingerprint: the zinc and copper and cadmium of the old works, the lead from somewhere, the arsenic that turned up everywhere, all of it riding on the calcium and magnesium that the limestone gave to any water passing through it. It was the fingerprint his whole thesis was about. These two samples had none of it. They didn't have the contaminants and they didn't have the limestone. Calcium, in water that had come out of a mountain made of calcium, was indistinguishable from the blank.

He ran the isotopes while he was still telling himself that the mass spectrometer needed recalibrating. Six injections from each vial into the laser analyzer, the way the method said. Spring meltwater ought to have come out light, close to the snow it had been a week ago. The groundwater in this district sat along its own tidy line on his plots, and he knew where on that line any sample from the Shulgan basin should fall. These two fell nowhere near it. They didn't look like snow, and they didn't look like rain, or like any water source he had a number for. And the two vials agreed with each other. One had come from the channel outside and the other from above the pale stone, minutes and meters apart. They matched to the last digit the instrument reported, injection after injection, with less scatter between them than the instrument was supposed to be capable of. He reran the laboratory standard to check. The standard scattered the way it always did, a little this way, a little that. The samples didn't scatter at all.

He sat back on the stool.

Every way of cleaning water that he knew of left a mark. Membranes took out some things and not others. Distillation shifted the isotopes, always in the same direction. Ion exchange left its own residue. You could look at a treated sample and read what had been done to it the way you could read a repaired coat. This had no marks. It did not look like water that had been cleaned. It looked like a set of results after you'd finished correcting them, once you'd subtracted the blank and taken out the drift and the matrix effects and every bit of noise the world had added. All of that he did to data every day, on a screen, at the end of a run. It looked as if someone had run the corrections on the water itself.

He did not like that thought, and he got up and went to the sink to wash his hands, mostly to have something to do with them.

The burn on the inside of his left wrist had been there since his first year, from an afternoon with nitric acid and a pipette he had been too impatient to wait for: a pale, shiny patch about the size of a coin, a little puckered at one edge. He looked at it under the buzzing tube while the water ran. It seemed smaller. Or it seemed paler, closer to the color of the skin around it. He could not have said which, and he realized he could not remember the last time he had actually looked at it. Years, maybe. He pressed it with his thumb, and it felt as it always did, which was like nothing in particular. He dried his hands and looked again and decided it was the light. Then he looked a third time anyway.

It was twenty to one.

In the back of the field notebook, folded in among his lecture notes, was a photocopy of the sign-up sheet from a field day two years ago. Dr. Khasanov's number was written at the bottom in his own hand, with the words *for questions*. As far as Timur knew, nobody had ever used it.

He called before he could think of a reason not to.

It rang four times. "Yes," a voice said. It was quiet, and it did not sound like a man who had been asleep.

"Dr. Khasanov. I'm sorry, I know it's late. My name is Timur Yuldashev, I'm a graduate student in chemistry, you gave a lecture, two lectures, you won't remember—"

"You wrote things down."

Timur closed his mouth. Then he opened it again and told him. He told it badly, out of order, the numbers first because the numbers were the part he knew how to say, and then the channel and the slab and the gap, and then the room. Khasanov let him. He didn't interrupt. Once or twice Timur stopped to make sure the call hadn't dropped, and each time there was a small sound on the line, a breath, a shifting, enough to say go on.

When Timur ran out, Khasanov said, "The clear water. When it came out into the flood, did it mix?"

"Not at first. It held together for about a meter."

"And inside. The cold. Was it in the water or in the stone?"

Timur hadn't asked himself that. He thought about the back of his hand above the pale band, his knuckles aching. "The stone, I think. The water was colder over it."

"Did you hear anything?"

"No. Not hear." He found that he had put two fingers against his breastbone. "You felt it more than heard it. Three times."

There was a silence long enough that he took the phone from his ear to look at it. The call was still running. The seconds went on counting.

"Where," Khasanov said.

Timur told him: the forestry road, the shot-up sign, the survey post, the cut with the mesh hanging off it, the slab. Khasanov made him go through it twice.

"And you went in during the thaw."

"Yes."

"Hm," Khasanov said. It was impossible to tell what kind of hm it was. "Are you still at the laboratory?"

"Yes."

"Then write it all down tonight, before you go home. All of it, in order, even what seems stupid. Especially that. By tomorrow you'll have started tidying it." A pause. "I'll come to you in the morning. Nine?"

"Nine. Yes. Thank you. I'm sorry about the—"

"Go home after," Khasanov said. "Sleep." And he was gone.

Timur sat for a while with the phone in his hand. Then he opened the notebook to a clean page and began to write it down, the way he'd been told, in order. He got as far as the probe resetting the first time before the fans upstairs shut off for the night.

The quiet came all at once, as it did every night at one, and every night it surprised him. Without the thudding in the ducts the lab sounded enormous, the fluorescent tube buzzing to itself and nothing else. He wrote another line.

Beside him on the bench, the field spectrometer made a sound.

He had switched it off when he came in and left it upside down on a paper towel to dry. The sound came from it all the same: a low tone at the very bottom of hearing, so low that he felt it in the bench under his forearms before he was certain he heard it at all. Next to the spectrometer stood the second vial, the one from above the pale stone, still capped. As he watched, the water inside it trembled. Fine lines crossed its surface, set over set, the way they had crossed the water in the chamber, and the glass made a faint sound against the bench top, like a fingernail tapping very lightly and very fast.

It went on for perhaps five seconds. Then it stopped. The water in the vial lay flat. The tube buzzed.

Timur didn't move for some time. When he did, it was to pick up the spectrometer and turn it over. The screen was dark. It was off. He set it down again exactly where it had been, square on its paper towel, as though that mattered.

Then he looked at the clock over the door and wrote down the time, and because he had been told to put down even the things that seemed stupid, he wrote down the rest.
