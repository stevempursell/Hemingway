# PROLOGUE

## The White Vein

By the time Timur Yuldashev found the broken fence, the rain had worked through his jacket and settled into the cotton underneath. He tugged at his collar, freeing the wet fabric from his neck.

The runoff channel ran below a limestone shelf, a row of concrete posts leaning at different angles along the old survey road. The road had stopped being a road years ago. Birch saplings grew through its joints, and this year's thaw had taken the gravel downslope. From below the shelf came a noise like heavy traffic that never thinned out.

"Tell me that's not where we're going," Alina said.

"What? The map says it's safe."

Timur unfolded his notebook. Rain had softened the edges of the pages, and his pencil dented the paper before it left a mark. He found the channel with the pencil's tip and pointed to the word written beside it in small, upright letters. *Safe.*

Alina looked at the word, then down at the noise coming up from below the shelf, then back at the word. "What is that, a secondhand map?"

"Hey. It's from a famed professor." A pause. "I think."

It was secondhand, strictly speaking. Dr. Khasanov had handed out copies at the end of a guest lecture two years ago: a Soviet survey sheet photocopied so many times that the contour lines had gone furry, gone over first in his pencil, with arrows along the drainage, remarks in that small upright hand, and some of the printed elevations crossed out and corrected. Last autumn Timur had checked one of the corrections with a borrowed GPS. The Soviet surveyors had the ridge wrong by more than the height of the chemistry building. Khasanov hadn't been off at all. Whether that made him famed was another matter. Most of the department knew him as the man who talked about river spirits.

Alina was looking at the broken fence. "We're not supposed to be past that. You know that. No permit, no supervisor."

"It's broken," Timur said. "I'm taking that as a yes." He had waited weeks for someone in the department to sign a form, and somewhere along the way he had decided that rules like these were mostly suggestions about how fast science ought to go. "Twenty minutes. A few readings, and we're back on the road."

"I'm here for the field hours," Alina said. "And because you're paying for the petrol. I want that on the record."

"Noted."

He went down the bank mostly on his backside, one hand raking through wet fern for something to hold, the other keeping the sample case up out of the mud. When his boots hit the rock ledge at the bottom he was already laughing, because there it was, exactly where the map had promised: the whole spring thaw roaring through the cut, an overhang caged in rusted mesh at the far end where the flood went into the mountain and didn't come out, and beside it the grated mouth of an old survey tunnel, bleeding a thin orange stain into the current. The stain was what he had come for. Soviet survey crews had tunneled under half the ridges in this district and left the tunnels to drain into whatever ran past, and following where that drainage went underground was the whole of his thesis.

He dropped to his knees on the ledge and pulled the probe out of the case to start on the stain, and that was when he saw the strip of clear water.

It came in from the side, out of a dark gap behind a fallen slab of rock, and joined the flood at an angle. The flood was the color of wet cement. The strip was clear as window glass, so clear that he could see the stones on the bottom under it, every edge of them, as if there were no water there at all. And it would not mix. It ran on beside the gray current for the better part of a meter with a clean edge to it, like oil sitting in water, except that it was water sitting in water, until the flood finally tore it apart.

It should have been pretty. He didn't like looking at it.

Alina came down the bank behind him the sensible way, sideways, one careful boot at a time, swearing quietly whenever the mud moved under her. She stopped beside him, saw where he was looking, and didn't say anything at all, which for Alina was a considerable statement.

"Spring water doesn't come out like that," he said. "Not in a flood. Not out of this rock." Anything coming out of these hills in April should have been as brown and murky as everything else, full of mud and leaves and dissolved stone. "That's cleaner than tap water."

"So it's a spring. Somebody's old pipe. Test it and let's go."

He put the probe into the gray water first, to be fair to it. The number on the screen sat where it always sat in these mountains, high, because every stream in the southern Urals came down full of the rock it ran through. Then he moved it into the clear strip.

The number began to fall. It fell steadily toward nothing, toward the reading you'd expect from water that had been boiled and filtered and boiled again. Then the screen went black. A second later it lit up with the manufacturer's logo and a cheerful little chime: it had decided, on its own, to start over.

"Oh, come on."

"What did it do?"

"It died and came back." He waited through the start-up, thinking about the eleven-page form he'd filled in when the last probe went into a river, and tried again. Gray water: high, normal. Clear water: falling, falling, then frozen on a number so low that he pulled the probe out before it could embarrass itself any further.

"Your probe is old," Alina said.

"Everything we own is old."

He filled a sample bottle from the clear strip, holding it under until the last bubble rose, capped it, and wrote the date and *side flow* on the label in grease pencil. When he held it up against the sky, he could not find a single speck in it.

"Wonderful," Alina said. "Bring it to the car. You can be fascinated in the car." She pointed downstream, where a dead branch had snagged on a ridge of rock. When they'd arrived it had been clear of the water. Now the water was dragging at it. "It's rising."

It was. Under the roar there was a deeper note now, the sound of a whole valley's snow arriving at once.

But a thin mist lay on the clear water where it came out from behind the slab, and the mist was moving the wrong way. The wind was blowing down the cut, into their faces, and the mist was drifting toward them anyway, out of the dark. Timur pulled off a glove and held his bare hand up to the gap. Cold air was pouring out of it, steady and hard, colder than the rain, colder than the river, as if a door had been left open on a winter much deeper than this one.

"Is that a Khasanov thing?" Alina said. "That's a Khasanov thing you're doing with your hand."

"It's breathing out," he said. "There's a big space back there."

"There's a big space in my grandmother's flat, and tonight it's going to have my entire family and a cake in it. I'd like to be one of the family. Ideally the cake makes it too."

"Fifteen minutes. I just want to see where it's coming from."

"Ten. And you carry the case back up the bank, and if I'm late, you explain it to my grandmother. In person. She'll want to look at you."

"Done."

He tied a line off on one of the old mesh bolts that still had a grip on the rock and leaned back on it until he believed it. Then he got down on his hands and knees in the clear, cold water and put his head into the gap behind the slab.

Behind him he heard Alina say something to the sky in Tatar that her grandmother would not have approved of, and then the splash of her coming after him.

The gap behind the slab was wider than it had looked from outside and lower than he would have liked. For the first few meters he could crawl, the water up to his elbows, the sample case dragging on its strap and knocking his heels whenever the current caught it. Then the ceiling came down and he had to go onto his belly, and the cold came in through the zip at his chest all at once and took his breath away. He lay there for a second with his mouth open, making a small noise he was glad nobody could hear over the water. Then he went on.

Every few meters he stopped to clip a plastic arrow onto the line, pointing back the way they had come.

"How far?" Alina called. Her voice came to him flattened against the rock.

"Not far."

"You don't know that."

"No."

The ceiling dropped again, until there was only a hand's width of air between the water and the rock, and he had to turn his face sideways to keep his mouth and nose in it. His helmet lamp lit a rough gray ceiling a few centimeters from his eye, beaded with drops that shook every time he breathed out. He pulled himself forward along the bottom by his fingertips. Behind him the case scraped and bumped. He thought, very clearly, about the eleven-page form, and whether the department would make him fill in another one if he drowned and the probe survived. Then the ceiling lifted a little, and a little more, and he could raise his head.

It took him a while to notice how quiet it had become. The roar of the channel had been behind them the whole way, pushing up the passage after them, and somewhere in the low part it had shrunk to a hum and then to almost nothing. Now he could hear his own breathing, loud and wet inside his hood, and the small slap of water on stone, and Alina somewhere behind him telling him, under her breath and without much conviction, that she hated him.

The water had changed too. At the entrance it had still carried a little grit, specks drifting through his lamp beam like dust in a sunlit window. Here there were fewer. A meter on, fewer still. By the time the passage was wide enough for him to get up onto his knees, he could see his gloved hands resting on the bottom and every pebble under them, sharp as if they were lying on a table in open air. He had stopped moving to look at them.

"Timur."

"Sorry."

He crawled the last stretch and came up into the chamber.

It was not large. His lamp found the far wall at perhaps eight meters and the ceiling just above his head, and the floor was under water deep enough to reach his thighs when he stood. The walls were black limestone, wet and gleaming. The water lay on the floor so still and so clear that for a moment the room seemed to have no floor at all, only black rock going down and down under the light.

Alina came up beside him, breathing hard. Her lamp swung across his and away, and that was how they both saw it.

A band of pale stone ran across the floor of the chamber, under the water, from one wall nearly to the other. It was a little wider than his outstretched arms and the color of candle wax, or very old ivory, and clear water was sliding over it. He could only tell the water was moving by the way the light shifted on the pale surface underneath. Thin streams came out of hairline cracks in the black rock on either side and crossed the floor toward the walls, where they vanished into the darker water at the edges.

He waded toward it. The cold reached him before he got there, a deeper cold than the one he had brought in with him from the crawl. When he crouched and held the back of his glove just over the water above the band, his knuckles began to ache.

He had expected quartz, because pale veins in dark rock are nearly always quartz. But quartz glitters and breaks into flat little faces, and this was as smooth as a bar of soap. It was laid down in layers that folded softly over one another, like the skin his grandmother used to lift off a pot of boiled milk with a spoon. There was no seam where the black rock met it. One simply became the other, like ink bleeding into wet paper.

"Timur." Alina had not come any closer. "Bottle. Then out."

He put the probe into the water over the band.

The number dropped the way it had outside, and this time he let it. It went down to almost nothing and stayed there, trembling in its last digit: water with nothing in it at all. Water that had come through a mountain of limestone and taken none of the mountain with it. He laughed. It came out short and ugly and bounced back at him off the ceiling, and he stopped.

"What?"

"There's nothing in it."

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know. It means there's nothing in it." He might as well have dipped the probe into the purified water the lab kept in the corner for experiments, the tap with the warning sign that people used for their coffee anyway.

His free hand was flat on the rock beside the band to steady himself, and that was where he felt it first. He didn't hear anything. It was a trembling in the stone under his palm, so faint that he took it at first for his own pulse, or his own shivering. Then it was more than that. On the surface of the water, under his lamp, fine ripples appeared in straight lines and began to slide. They crossed one another, set after set, until for a second or two the whole surface above the pale band was ruled into tiny squares, like graph paper drawn on water. Then it smoothed away, and the water lay still and clear as if nothing had touched it.

Across the chamber, Alina had gone quiet. He could hear her not breathing.

"Timur," she said. Just his name, very flat. "Tell me that was you."

"It wasn't me."

"Then we're leaving."

The second one came before he could answer. It was deeper and longer, and this time he didn't feel it in his hand first. He felt it in his chest, in the bone in the middle of his chest, a pressure that went through him and out the other side, too low for his ears to make much of, though his teeth knew all about it. His ears popped, both at once, as they did in a lift.

Behind them, the passage roared.

They turned. The low, quiet mouth they had crawled out of, with its hand's width of air, was churning now. Gray flood water was shoving in through it in heavy surges. The clear water of the chamber went cloudy around the opening as the mud came in, and the line he had laid ran out into that and disappeared.

Alina was already moving.

He filled the second bottle without really deciding to, holding it down in the clear water over the band until it was full, and capped it. Through his glove the glass was so cold it hurt. He was shoving it into the case when the water moved around his legs. For a moment all of it, the whole still floor of it, was flowing backward, away from the entrance and toward the far wall, hard enough to tip him forward. Then it stopped, snapped back, and came at the entrance so hard it nearly took his feet out from under him.

On the line, where it ran out into the churning passage, the little plastic arrows had all swung sideways. They hung crosswise in the current, pointing at the walls instead of the way out.

Alina went down. She was a step ahead of him, reaching for the line, and the water took her legs. She vanished under it to her shoulders and came up spitting, one hand locked on the line and the other clawing at the wall. He got hold of her harness and hauled, and she found her feet and swore at him, or at the cave, in two languages.

"Go," he said. "You first. Keep your hand on the line."

The passage had closed in. Where there had been a hand's width of air in the low part, there were now a few fingers, and the water in it was surging, shoving forward and dragging back, so that getting through was like being passed back and forth between two people who couldn't agree. He turned his face up into the air pocket and pulled himself along the line, the case banging his legs. The cold was so complete that he had stopped feeling it as cold. He felt it as slowness now, as hands that didn't close when he told them to. Ahead of him Alina's boots kicked and stopped and kicked. Once he heard her shoulder hit rock and a cry that the water swallowed.

The third one came in the worst of it, with his face pressed to the ceiling and the water at his lips. He felt it in his ribs and his teeth and in the rock against his cheek, and for one moment he had the idea, which he would never have said out loud, that the whole mountain had drawn in a breath and was holding it. Then the thought was gone, and there was only the line in his hand and the next pull and the next.

Daylight came at them gray and sudden, and they spilled out under the slab into the rain.

The channel was roaring exactly as before. The dead branch was underwater now. Up the slope the birches tossed in the wind. Alina crawled into the shallows on her hands and knees and stayed there a while with her head down, breathing. Then she got up all at once and stood holding her left shoulder with her right hand.

"I'm fine," she said, before he could ask. "Don't."

He turned to look at the gap behind the slab. The side stream was still coming out of it into the main current, and it was gray, as gray and muddy as everything else, and it went into the flood without any edge at all and was lost at once. The mist was gone with it.

He looked for a long time, until Alina said his name. Then he cut the line at the mesh bolt with his knife, because he could not make himself go back under the slab to reel it in, and stuffed the loose end into his pocket. The case dragged at his shoulder. Through its side, against his hip, he could feel the two bottles, very cold.

Alina was already climbing toward the road. "You owe me petrol," she said, without looking back, "and a shoulder, and an explanation for my grandmother. And it was twelve minutes. I counted."

***

The chemistry building at night smelled of floor polish and old radiators, and of something underneath both that had been spilled some time in the last century and never entirely left. The watchman in the glass booth by the doors looked up from his little television long enough to recognize Timur, take in his wet hair and the case on his shoulder, and decide that none of it was his business. That was the arrangement they had come to over three years, and Timur was grateful for it.

He changed into the dry clothes he kept in his locker for nights when he missed the last bus. His hands still smelled of the cave. On the fourth floor the ventilation fans thudded away in the ducts overhead, as they did every night until one, like someone in the attic moving furniture who could never decide where it should go. One of the lights over his bench buzzed. It had buzzed since the autumn. He had filed a request about it, and then a second request about the first one.

The first test was the simplest: how much of anything was dissolved in the water. River water carries the land it runs through, every stream its own pinch of salt and stone. He wanted, more than he would have admitted, for the probe to have been wrong.

It hadn't been. The sample read like the purified water the lab made for itself in the corner, the water that went through three filters and an ultraviolet lamp before anyone was allowed to use it, and it went on reading that way. He stood over it with his arms folded and waited for it to change. Water that clean never stays clean for long. It starts picking things up from the air and the glass almost at once. This had been sitting in a bottle in a car for five hours, and it was still as clean as the moment he'd filled it.

He ran the metals next, on the big machine down the hall that had arrived in a crate stenciled with Deka's logo and came with a long agreement about who was allowed to touch it and when. He was supposed to book it a week ahead. He did not book it. He loaded both bottles behind someone's abandoned soil samples and went back to his bench to wait.

The vending machine on the landing had been dead since February. He ate a packet of crackers from the back of his drawer and opened his Deka quarterly report, looked for some time at the box at the bottom marked *Community Engagement*, typed *Engaged* and deleted it. His phone buzzed. Alina had sent a photograph of the cake, intact, with her grandmother's hand cutting into it and a lot of elbows around the edges. A minute later: *shoulder purple. you owe me.* And a minute after that: *what was it*

He typed *don't know yet* and then sat looking at the words until the screen went dark.

The metals came through a little after half past eleven. Since September he had sampled more than two hundred streams and seeps in this district, and every one of them told the same story in its numbers: the rock it had passed through, and on top of the rock, the poison the old Soviet mines had left behind, the heavy metals that his whole thesis was about following underground. These two bottles told no story at all. There was no poison in them. There was no rock in them either. This was water that had come out of a limestone mountain with no limestone in it, like tea with no tea.

The last test was the one that tells you where water comes from. Rain and snow carry a faint signature of where and when they fell, and Timur knew where on his chart the water of this valley ought to land. These two landed nowhere near it, and nowhere near anything else he had ever measured. And they matched each other. One had come from the channel outside and the other from over the pale stone, minutes and meters apart, and they agreed with each other more closely than the machine was supposed to be able to measure, run after run. He tested the lab's reference sample to make sure the machine was behaving. The reference wobbled a little from run to run, as it always did. His samples didn't wobble at all.

He sat back on the stool and tried to think like a chemist.

Every way of cleaning water he had ever heard of left a mark. Filters let some things through. Boiling and condensing shifted the water's signature in a known direction. Every treatment left behind traces of whatever it had done, and you could read a cleaned sample the way you could read a mended coat. This had no marks. It didn't look like water that had been cleaned. It looked like his results at the end of a long day, after he'd corrected them, after he'd subtracted the background and the drift and every bit of noise the world had added on the way to the instrument. It looked as though the corrections had been made in the water itself.

He didn't like that thought. He got up and went to the sink to wash his hands, mostly so they would have something to do.

The burn on the inside of his left wrist had been there since his first year, from an afternoon with nitric acid and a pipette he'd been too impatient to wait for. It was a pale, shiny patch about the size of a coin, puckered at one edge. He looked at it under the buzzing light while the water ran. It seemed smaller. Or paler, closer to the color of the skin around it. He couldn't have said which, and he realized he couldn't remember the last time he had actually looked at it. Years, maybe. He pressed it with his thumb, and it felt as it always did, which was like nothing in particular. He dried his hands, looked again, and decided it was the light. Then he looked a third time anyway.

At the bottom of Khasanov's photocopied map, under the furry contour lines, was a telephone number and the word *questions*. It was twenty to one in the morning. As far as Timur knew, nobody had ever used the number, and he called it before he could think of a reason not to.

It rang four times. "Yes," a voice said. It was quiet, and it didn't sound like a man who had been asleep.

"Dr. Khasanov. I'm sorry, I know how late it is. My name is Timur Yuldashev, I'm a graduate student in chemistry. You gave a lecture here two years ago. You won't remember me."

"No," Khasanov agreed, without any unkindness. "Tell me anyway."

So Timur told him. He told it badly and out of order, the numbers first because the numbers were the part he knew how to say, then the channel and the slab and the gap, and then the room. Khasanov didn't interrupt. Once or twice Timur stopped to make sure the call hadn't dropped, and each time there was some small sound on the line, a breath, the creak of a chair, enough to say *go on*.

When Timur ran out, Khasanov said, "The clear water. When it came out into the flood, did it mix?"

"Not at first. It held together for about a meter."

"And inside. The cold. Was it in the water or in the stone?"

Timur hadn't asked himself that. He thought about the back of his hand over the pale band, his knuckles aching. "The stone, I think. The water was colder over it."

"Did you hear anything?"

"No. Not hear." He found he had put two fingers against his breastbone. "You felt it more than heard it. Three times."

The line went quiet for long enough that he took the phone from his ear and looked at it. The call was still running, the seconds still counting.

"Where is it?" Khasanov said.

Timur told him: the forestry road, the fallen fence, the cut with the mesh hanging off it, the slab. Khasanov made him go through it twice.

"And you went in. During the thaw."

"Yes."

"Then you're braver than you are sensible," Khasanov said. "That's normal at your age. It wears off." Timur couldn't tell whether he was smiling. "Are you still at the laboratory?"

"Yes."

"Then write it down tonight, before you go home. All of it, in order. Even the parts that seem stupid. I'll come to you in the morning. Nine?"

"Nine. Yes. Thank you. I'm sorry about the—"

"Go home after," Khasanov said. "Sleep." And he was gone.

Timur sat for a while with the phone in his hand. Then he opened the notebook to a clean page and began to write it down, as he'd been told, in order. He had got as far as the probe restarting itself for the first time when the fans upstairs shut off for the night. The quiet came all at once, as it did every night at one, and every night it surprised him. Without the thudding in the ducts the lab sounded enormous, the buzzing light talking to itself and nothing else. He wrote another line.

Beside him on the bench, the field spectrometer made a sound.

He had switched it off when he came in and left it upside down on a paper towel to dry. The sound came from it anyway: a low tone at the very bottom of hearing, so low that he felt it in the bench under his forearms before he was sure he heard it at all. Next to the spectrometer stood the second bottle, the one from over the pale stone, still capped. As he watched, the water inside it trembled. Fine lines crossed its surface, set over set, like the lines that had crossed the water in the chamber, and the glass made a faint sound against the bench top, a fingernail tapping very lightly and very fast.

It went on for perhaps five seconds. Then it stopped. The water in the bottle lay flat. The light buzzed.

Timur didn't move for some time. When he did, it was to pick up the spectrometer and turn it over. The screen was dark. It was off. He set it down again exactly where it had been, square on its paper towel, as though that mattered.

Then he looked at the clock over the door and wrote down the time, and because he had been told to put down even the parts that seemed stupid, he wrote down the rest.
