# PROLOGUE

## The White Vein

By the time Timur Yuldashev found the broken fence, the rain had worked through his jacket and settled into the cotton underneath. He tugged at his collar, freeing the wet fabric from his neck.

The runoff channel ran below a limestone shelf, a row of concrete posts leaning at different angles along the old survey road. The road had stopped being a road years ago. Birch saplings grew through its joints, and this year's thaw had taken the gravel downslope. From below the shelf came a noise like heavy traffic that never thinned out.

"Tell me that's not where we're going," Alina said.

Timur unfolded his notebook. Rain had softened the edges of the pages, and his pencil dented the paper before it left a mark. He drew a line across the rough map, connecting the culvert to the nearest surveyed drainage passage.

"We're here," he said.

Alina leaned in. "You're using his map again."

"It's a geological survey."

It was a geological survey with Khasanov's handwriting all over it. Dr. Khasanov had handed out copies at the end of a guest lecture two years ago, a Soviet survey sheet photocopied so many times that the contour lines had gone furry, and he had gone over it in pencil first: arrows along the drainage, remarks in tiny upright letters, and some of the printed elevations crossed out and corrected. Last autumn Timur had checked one of the corrections with a borrowed GPS. The Soviet surveyors had been off by eleven meters. Khasanov hadn't been off at all.

"The map says it's safe," Timur said. He found the channel with the tip of his pencil and pointed to the word written beside it in the same small, upright hand. *Safe.*

Alina looked at the word, then down at the noise coming up from below the shelf, then back at the word. "What is that, a secondhand map?"

"Hey. It's from a famed professor." A pause. "I think."

He tapped the margin, where Khasanov had written *where does the water go* and circled it three times. Then he closed the notebook. "An excellent question for a hydrologist."

"You're a chemist," Alina said.

"Which is why I only need twenty minutes. I take a few readings, we go home, and you're at your grandmother's by eight with a cake that's still a cake."

The cake was the second most important passenger in Alina's mother's car. It was strapped into the back seat with a seatbelt, because Alina did not trust the forestry road. Her grandmother turned seventy tonight, and thirty relatives would be packed into a ninth-floor flat by eight o'clock. Alina had come only because Timur was paying the petrol and she needed field hours, and she had told him both of these things several times. Neither of them was supposed to be here at all. The reserve wanted a permit for anyone who stepped off the road, and the department wanted a supervisor on any fieldwork during the flood. Timur had decided, around the third week of waiting for a signature, that both rules were suggestions about how fast science ought to go.

He went down the bank mostly on his backside, one hand raking through wet fern for something to hold, the other keeping the sample case up out of the mud. When his boots hit the rock ledge at the bottom he was already laughing, because there it was, exactly where the map had promised. A cut in the pale limestone with the whole spring thaw roaring through it. At the far end, an overhang caged in rusted mesh, with the grated mouth of an old survey tunnel beside it, where the water went into the mountain and did not come out.

He dropped to his knees on the ledge and pulled the probe out of the case, and that was when he saw the strip of clear water.

It came in from the side, out of a dark gap behind a fallen slab of rock, and joined the flood at an angle. The flood was the color of wet cement. The strip was clear as window glass, so clear that he could see the stones on the bottom under it, every edge of them, as if there were no water there at all. And it would not mix. It ran on beside the gray current for the better part of a meter with a clean edge to it, like oil sitting in water, except that it was water sitting in water, until the flood finally tore it apart.

It should have been pretty. He didn't like looking at it.

Alina came down the bank behind him the sensible way, sideways, one careful boot at a time, swearing quietly whenever the mud moved under her. She stopped beside him, saw where he was looking, and didn't say anything at all, which for Alina was a considerable statement.

"Spring water doesn't come out like that," he said. "Not in a flood. Not out of this rock." Anything coming out of these hills in April should have been as brown and murky as everything else, full of mud and leaves and dissolved stone. "That's cleaner than tap water."

"So it's a spring. Somebody's old pipe. Test it and let's go."

He put the probe into the gray water first, to be fair to it. The number on the screen sat where it always sat in these mountains, high, because every stream in the southern Urals came down full of the rock it ran through. Then he moved it into the clear strip.

The number began to fall. It fell steadily toward nothing, toward the reading you'd expect from water that had been boiled and filtered and boiled again. Then the screen went black. A second later it lit up with the manufacturer's logo and a cheerful little chime: it had decided, on its own, to start over.

"Oh, come on."

"What did it do?"

"It died and came back." He waited through the start-up, thinking about the eleven-page form he'd filled in when the last probe went into a river, and tried again. Gray water: high, normal. Clear water: falling, falling, then frozen on a number so low that he pulled the probe out before it could embarrass itself any further.

"Your probe is old," Alina said.

"Everything we own is old."

He filled a sample bottle from the clear strip, holding it under until the last bubble rose, capped it, and wrote on the label in grease pencil: the date, *T-14*, *side flow under slab*. When he held it up against the sky, he could not find a single speck in it.

"Wonderful," Alina said. "Bring it to the car. You can be fascinated in the car." She pointed downstream, where a dead branch had snagged on a ridge of rock. When they'd arrived it had been clear of the water. Now the water was dragging at it. "It's rising."

It was. Under the roar there was a deeper note now, the sound of a whole valley's snow arriving at once.

But a thin mist lay on the clear water where it came out from behind the slab, and the mist was moving the wrong way. The wind was blowing down the cut, into their faces, and the mist was drifting toward them anyway, out of the dark. Timur pulled off a glove and held his bare hand up to the gap. Cold air was pouring out of it, steady and hard, colder than the rain, colder than the river, as if a door had been left open on a winter much deeper than this one.

"Is that a Khasanov thing?" Alina said. "That's a Khasanov thing you're doing with your hand."

"It's breathing out," he said. "There's a big space back there."

"There's a big space in my grandmother's flat, and in about four hours it'll have thirty people and a cake in it. I'd like to be one of the people. Ideally the cake makes it too."

"Fifteen minutes. I just want to see where it's coming from."

"Ten. And you carry the case back up the bank, and if I'm late, you explain it to my grandmother. In person. She'll want to look at you."

"Done."

He tied a line off on one of the old mesh bolts that still had a grip on the rock and leaned back on it until he believed it. Then he got down on his hands and knees in the clear, cold water and put his head into the gap behind the slab.

Behind him he heard Alina say something to the sky in Tatar that her grandmother would not have approved of, and then the splash of her coming after him.
