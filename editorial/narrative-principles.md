# Narrative Principles

> The author's standing instructions (from the Fiction Writing Agent V2 brief, 2026-10-14), made operational. These apply to every chapter. Revise them only through `record-feedback`.

## 1. Scenes, not summaries
- A beat sheet lists events that **must occur**. It is not a list of paragraphs to produce.
- Never write a beat, acknowledge it, and move straight on. Let events unfold: give characters time to deal with their environment, decide, misunderstand each other, and respond to complications.
- Routine activity, humor, awkwardness, quiet moments, and incidental conversation are **welcome**. Don't cut them just because they don't advance the plot. They build character, atmosphere, and immersion.
- The story should often feel like something happening *while the characters are busy living their lives*.

## 2. Narrative restraint (the most important principle)
The writer knows the whole series. The characters don't. Before every scene, answer in the plan:
1. What does each character know at this moment?
2. What is each character concerned about, plot aside?
3. How do these people actually relate to one another?
4. What ordinary things occupy their attention?
5. What would they naturally discuss here?

**The test for every line: "Does this feel authentic to these characters at this moment in their lives?"**

- The plot can unfold *alongside* conversation. It doesn't have to be the subject of it.
- **No conspicuous setup.** Dialogue doesn't foreshadow. Characters don't introduce concepts just because the bible contains them. Nobody explains why a person, place, object, or historical event matters. Details whose significance comes later get no spotlight now.
- Foreshadowing is subtle and arises from circumstance. A strange observation can pass without anyone declaring what it means. **Let significance accumulate.**
- **Knowing a detail is not an obligation to show it.** Facts from the plan, the bible, or the author's answers (gear, procedures, backstory, logistics) appear on the page only when the POV character would notice or use them *in that moment*. Never write a passage to justify a detail's presence or to show the research. Never explain what an object is for before it matters. Plans settle logistics so the prose can leave them out.

## 3. Author lines vs. illustrations
- **Lines he gives in chat are usable** (2026-10-14: "If I give you lines you can use them."). Weave them in with light continuity edits (POV naming, tense), and log them in the draft notes.
- **Illustrations are not text:** anything he presents as an example of a principle, and the beat sheets' sample lines (confirmed as illustrations, P2). Learn the principle, then write original prose.
- Don't over-correct. Rejecting his good lines out of caution is as much a failure as pasting an illustration. If unsure, use it and flag it.

## 4. Prose and reading experience
- Engaging commercial fiction for adult readers: clarity, immersion, character, momentum.
- Brevity isn't automatically a virtue. Neither is elaborate description. Pick the level of detail the scene needs.
- Vary sentence and paragraph length naturally.
- Humor comes from character and situation.
- Dialogue can have interruptions, misunderstandings, digressions, subtext. No polished thematic statements.
- **Don't** end paragraphs by reflex with ominous observations, philosophical summaries, or manufactured revelations.
- **Don't** use clipped sentences and isolated one-line paragraphs to fake intensity.
- **Don't** inflate prose to hit a word count.
- It should be enjoyable sentence by sentence, not just competent at delivering plot.
- Review question: **does it read like a novel, or like an expanded synopsis?**

## 5. Mechanical patterns to avoid (reviewer counts these)
- Paragraphs ending on an ominous or epigrammatic "button."
- "Not X. Y." / "not X, but Y" reveals; "something [adjective]" vagueness ("something enormous," "something wrong").
- Tricolons and rule-of-three lists by reflex.
- Characters "noticing" things purely to show them to the reader.
- Repeated sentence openings; stacked adverbs ("strangely," "unnaturally," "impossibly" on repeat).
- Similes that explain rather than show. Similes drawn from outside the POV character's world.
- Emotions stated outright, then demonstrated anyway.
- Over-precise stage directions for every gesture.

## 6. Scientific and physical honesty
- **Accessible first (author, 2026-10-14):** the science has to land for a reader who knows nothing about water, chemistry, caves, or weather. The reader should feel what a finding *means* (impossible, wrong, dangerous) without needing to know how it was measured. Translate every technical fact into a plain consequence the POV character feels, or cut it. Numbers, units, and instrument names are rare spice, not structure.
- **Still accurate:** nothing on the page may be wrong for a specialist. Accuracy lives under the prose, in what's chosen and how characters react, not in explanation.
- Hard SF: instruments, chemistry, caving, cold-water physiology, and travel times should survive scrutiny from a specialist. When the science has to bend (the Resonator), the *observation* stays precise even if the explanation is missing.
- The Resonator never explains itself, communicates, or becomes anthropomorphic. Its biological effects are subtle and statistical: no instant healing, no fountain of youth.

## 7. Length
- Chapters run 3,000–5,000 words by default. Prologues can vary. Budget each scene during planning. Report actual counts.
- Don't pad with repetitive description, redundant dialogue, or exposition. Don't compress meaningful scenes to finish faster.

## 8. Using published fiction
Craft analysis only (scene construction, pacing, dialogue, exposition, POV, suspense, genre expectations). Never reproduce passages. Never imitate a living author's distinctive voice. Never import recognizable characters, plots, or sequences. The author's own voice comes first.
