# PROLOGUE
## The White Vein

Timur Yuldashev stood at the edge of the runoff channel and tried unsuccessfully to keep rainwater from dripping into the sample case hanging against his hip and, less importantly, down the back of his neck and beneath his second-hand raincoat. The seal on the sample case lid had already begun to warp from the cold. He made a mental note to complain about it in the equipment report he would never actually submit.

By late April the southern Ural Mountains stopped pretending winter still had authority over them. Snowmelt came down through the forested slopes in violent gray sheets, carrying half-frozen branches, loose shale, and the accumulated filth of abandoned roads into the river systems below. Every culvert overflowed. Every ditch had become a stream. Water moved through the mountains with heavy, continuous presence.

Below him, runoff rushed through a fractured limestone cut and vanished beneath an overhang reinforced decades earlier with Soviet-era steel mesh now furred orange with rust. Most of the retaining bolts had pulled free from the rock. One section of fencing leaned outward over the torrent at an angle suggesting it would not survive another season. He had seen this before. Most industrial work in his home region eventually softened and sagged into the landscape once the money disappeared. Concrete cracked. Steel bloomed orange. Entire facilities remained standing long after everyone quietly agreed they no longer functioned.

Alina climbed carefully down the embankment behind him, boots slipping in the mud.

"If we die doing runoff chemistry," she said, breathing hard, "I want it understood this was your idea."

Timur crouched near the waterline without looking back at her.

"You came voluntarily."

"I came because Deka pays triple rates for field certification hours."

"That still sounds voluntary."

"That sounds like economic coercion."

Timur smiled faintly and unclipped the handheld probe from his kit. The university's chemistry department had spent the better part of three years pretending Deka's funding did not effectively keep several research programs alive. Officially, faculty still referred to the corporation as an industrial partner. Unofficially, everyone understood the distinction between partnership and dependency had disappeared some time ago.

He lowered the probe into the runoff.

For several seconds the readings behaved normally. Conductivity stabilized within expected range while mineral concentrations scrolled steadily across the cracked display. Then the numbers began drifting downward. Dissolved solids dropped sharply before recovering again. Trace mineral readings vanished altogether, returned briefly, then collapsed a second time.

"Equipment failure?" Alina asked.

"Probably moisture in the contacts."

"You say that every time something strange happens."

"Most strange things are equipment failures."

"Comforting philosophy for someone standing above a flooded cave system."

Timur ignored her and crouched lower beside the channel. Only then did he notice that the clearer water emerging from beneath the limestone shelf was not mixing properly with the surrounding runoff. A narrow tributary maintained its own transparency inside the larger current for almost a meter before finally dissolving into the gray sediment downstream.

That alone should not have been possible.

He removed a sample tube from the case and filled it carefully. Even beneath the weak overcast light the water looked unnaturally clean. Spring runoff carried sediment from everywhere—soil, limestone particulate, decaying vegetation washed downhill during thaw—but the sample tube contained almost nothing visible at all.

Rain ticked steadily against the hood of his jacket. Somewhere farther down the valley runoff hammered exposed stone with enough force to produce low rolling echoes through the trees.

Alina stepped beside him and followed his gaze toward the dark opening beneath the overhang.

"No," she said.

Timur glanced up at her. "I haven't said anything."

"You didn't need to."

Cold air moved steadily out from beneath the limestone shelf. Not wind exactly. More like pressure exchange from a larger enclosed space somewhere deeper underground. Timur watched runoff curl around broken stone and disappear into the darkness.

"There's probably an exposed spring chamber behind it," he said.

"There's probably a collapsed tunnel full of freezing water."

"Same thing."

"One of those descriptions sounds significantly worse."

Despite herself, Alina smiled briefly before shaking her head again.

"Khasanov specifically warned people about the old runoff channels during spring thaw."

"Khasanov warns people about weather patterns like they're moral choices."

"He also predicted the sinkhole collapse outside Kaga six months before the geological office did."

"That's because the geological office consists mostly of alcoholics."

"He thinks Bashkir folklore contains hydrological records."

Timur shrugged. "Maybe some of it does."

Alina looked at him with visible disappointment. "You took notes during his lectures, didn't you?"

"He's interesting."

"He compared aquifers to circulatory systems."

"He was making a systems argument."

"He spent twenty minutes talking about river spirits."

Timur clipped the secondary lamp to his shoulder harness and lowered himself toward the opening beneath the limestone shelf. Meltwater surged immediately around his boots, leaking through seams that had probably failed years earlier.

Behind him Alina exhaled loudly through her nose before following.

The opening narrowed quickly enough that both of them were forced nearly flat against the rock. Timur dragged the sample case behind him while runoff pushed hard against his forearms and chest. The roar of the flood outside diminished with surprising speed until only enclosed echoes remained, bursts of water carrying through unseen cavities somewhere deeper inside the mountain.

After several uncomfortable meters the restriction widened abruptly.

Timur crawled forward once more and emerged into a low chamber partially submerged beneath still black water. Their lamps moved slowly across slick limestone walls streaked with pale mineral deposits that disappeared beneath the surface. Water dripped intermittently from somewhere beyond the reach of the lights, though even those sounds seemed strangely muted by the shape of the chamber itself.

Alina bumped lightly into his shoulder before stopping beside him.

Near the center of the flooded space a pale formation cut through the darker limestone in a broad irregular band. Thin streams of perfectly clear water emerged from fractures surrounding it and crossed the chamber floor almost invisibly before disappearing into the darker runoff near the walls.

Timur stepped toward it slowly.

At first glance the formation resembled quartz intrusion, but the closer he moved the less convincing that explanation became. The surface lacked any crystalline structure. Instead it appeared smooth and layered, pale overlapping mineral folds embedded within the surrounding limestone as though the rock had formed around it rather than through it.

The temperature changed gradually near the formation. Cold seeped upward through the soles of Timur's boots while he knelt beside the water, subtle enough that he noticed it only after several seconds.

"Take your sample," Alina said quietly from near the entrance, "and let's leave."

Timur lowered the probe into the clear flow. The readings stabilized briefly before becoming erratic. Conductivity values accelerated downward while mineral concentrations fluctuated wildly across the display. A moment later the screen filled with corrupted characters dense enough to obscure the measurement grid entirely.

Timur tapped the side of the casing with his thumb, more irritated than concerned. Moisture probably had gotten into the contacts during the crawl through the runoff. The university bought field equipment the same way it repaired laboratory ventilation systems: reluctantly and several years too late.

As he adjusted the probe, a faint vibration moved through the limestone beneath his hand.

The disturbance spread outward through the chamber water almost immediately. Thin ripples crossed beneath the beam of his lamp in strangely regular lines, intersecting and folding across one another before gradually disappearing into the black surface near the walls.

Across the chamber Alina had stopped moving entirely.

"You felt that too."

Her voice remained quiet, controlled in the way people spoke when they had already decided something was wrong and saw no reason to make it worse by sounding frightened.

Another vibration followed, deeper and more sustained than the first. Timur felt it through his palm and then a moment later through his sternum, low enough that hearing barely registered it before the sensation passed through his body.

Behind them water struck stone with sudden force.

Both of them turned toward the restriction entrance.

The runoff passage had narrowed visibly. Gray floodwater surged inward now in heavy bursts that rattled loose fragments of mud and limestone from the ceiling.

"That wasn't happening before," Timur said.

"No," Alina replied. "It definitely was not."

He filled another sample tube quickly from the clear water beside the pale formation. Even through insulated gloves the glass carried an unnatural cold.

The chamber water shifted around his knees.

For one brief second the current reversed direction entirely before snapping back toward the runoff channel.

Alina swore sharply and moved toward the restriction.

Timur shoved the sample tube into the case and followed. The crawlspace had become a narrow throat of muddy floodwater churning hard enough against the limestone ceiling to spray freezing runoff back into their faces. He flattened himself sideways through the tightest section while the current twisted unpredictably around his legs, pulling backward one moment and sharply sideways the next.

Ahead of him Alina lost footing briefly and struck the wall shoulder-first with a muffled cry.

Another vibration rolled through the cave.

Timur felt it in his ribs and teeth while the entire chamber behind him seemed, for one deeply irrational moment, less like empty geology than something enormous shifting in uneasy sleep.

He forced himself forward before the thought could settle properly in his mind.

Cold daylight finally broke across the water ahead and they spilled back into open rain beneath the gray valley sky.

The river continued roaring through the limestone gorge exactly as before. Wind moved through the birch trees above the runoff channel. Nothing in the landscape suggested anything extraordinary existed beneath the mountain.

Alina bent forward with both hands on her knees, breathing hard.

"No," she said finally. "Absolutely no."

Timur turned back toward the overhang.

The narrow tributary emerging from beneath the limestone shelf had already clouded again with ordinary sediment-heavy runoff. The impossible transparency was gone.

Only the sample tubes remained cold against his hip.

Rain drifted quietly through the valley while neither of them spoke.

Then Alina straightened slowly and narrowed her eyes at him.

"You're thinking about going back."

Timur adjusted the sample case against his side.

"No," he said.

The lie arrived too quickly to convince either of them.
